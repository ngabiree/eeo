# EEO Endowment Ontology

## Purpose

This ontology defines the conceptual layers that guide EEO product, data, evidence, and public communication.

It is not a claim of ownership, legal authority, certification, or adjudication.

## Core EEO layers

### 1. Natural Endowment Layer

Documents the physical and ecological foundations of life.

Includes:

- water;
- rivers;
- lakes;
- aquifers;
- rainfall;
- watersheds;
- arable land;
- soil;
- forests;
- pasture;
- biodiversity;
- wildlife;
- minerals;
- energy resources;
- carbon sinks;
- ecological corridors;
- climate-regulating systems.

Primary questions:

- What exists?
- Where is it?
- What quantity is known or estimated?
- What is its quality or condition?
- Is it renewable, recyclable, seasonal, fragile, degraded, or non-renewable?
- What life-support function does it serve?

### 2. Human Dependency and Stewardship Layer

Shows how people live with, depend on, transform, protect, and are affected by Earth’s endowments.

This layer is not a surveillance layer, identity-ranking layer, ethnic map, or political-control layer.

It uses aggregated, rights-aware data.

Primary questions:

- Who depends on this endowment for water, food, land, energy, income, culture, or survival?
- How many people may be affected by depletion, pollution, drought, flooding, extraction, conservation, or policy failure?
- What livelihoods are connected to the endowment?
- Where does natural wealth coexist with poverty, weak infrastructure, or limited public benefit?
- How do communities, workers, consumers, states, and markets depend on one another?

Disclosure rule:

EEO must not expose individuals, vulnerable communities, sacred sites, ethnic identities, displaced populations, informal settlements, or sensitive local knowledge in ways that could enable harm, retaliation, exploitation, land grabbing, or political targeting.

### 3. Value Movement Layer

Follows how natural endowments become economic value.

Includes:

- extraction;
- stewardship;
- production;
- labor;
- processing;
- transport;
- trade;
- finance;
- ownership;
- taxation;
- public revenue;
- private profit;
- community benefit;
- ecological cost;
- waste;
- recycling;
- restoration.

Primary questions:

- How does value move from the endowment into the economy?
- Who works to transform the endowment?
- Where is it processed, transported, traded, consumed, or recycled?
- What public revenue is generated?
- What local benefits are visible?
- What ecological and social costs are created?
- Which regions depend on each other through trade, technology, food, water, energy, or minerals?

### 4. Governance, Evidence, and Safeguards Layer

Protects EEO’s credibility and public safety.

Includes:

- source;
- license;
- method;
- date;
- confidence label;
- uncertainty;
- legal posture;
- disclosure tier;
- review status;
- right-of-reply status;
- correction path;
- release manifest.

Primary questions:

- What evidence supports the claim?
- What is the source and date?
- What method was used?
- What is known, estimated, uncertain, or disputed?
- What should be public, aggregated, restricted, delayed, or withheld?
- Who has a right to correct, challenge, reply, or request safer disclosure?

## EEO public promise

EEO helps people see Earth’s endowments clearly: where they are, what they do, who depends on them, how they change, and how their value moves through society.

## Red lines

EEO must not present itself as:

- a world government;
- a global resource owner;
- a universal ownership registry;
- a redistribution engine;
- a legal tribunal;
- a certification system;
- an ESG score;
- a blockchain trust layer;
- an AI decision authority;
- a public exposure engine.

## Foundational phrase

From Earth to life, from life to economy, from economy to responsibility — made visible.
